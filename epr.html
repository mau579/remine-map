The big picture

What happens when we’re finished with a product?

Batteries, electronics, and packaging do not disappear after we use them. Someone still has to collect, transport, and properly manage those materials.

Extended Producer Responsibility—or EPR—is one way communities and producers can share that work.

What is EPR? ↓
↻

EPR made simple

What does EPR mean?

EPR keeps producers involved with certain products after they are sold.

01

Producers participate

Companies that place covered products on the market may help organize and fund their collection and recycling.

02

Communities receive support

Local programs may receive resources for transportation, equipment, education, and safe handling.

03

Residents get clearer options

A good program makes it easier to understand what is accepted, where it goes, and how to handle it safely.

Why it matters

Today, the responsibility is not evenly shared.

Residents are asked to recycle correctly, but they did not choose how a product was designed or whether a recovery system would exist.

  • ?Residents may not know where products belong.
  • $Local programs may carry collection and transportation costs.
  • ↘Valuable materials can be lost.
  • !Batteries may enter trash or curbside recycling and create safety risks.
  • ⌂Rural communities may have fewer convenient options.

EPR is intended to create a clearer way to share those responsibilities.

Why batteries?

Batteries are useful—but they need a clear way back.

01

Public safety

Some batteries can create fire risks when damaged or mixed with ordinary trash and curbside recycling.

02

Public confusion

Instructions vary by battery type and location. Many residents are unsure what is accepted or where to take it.

03

Material recovery

Batteries contain materials that may be recovered and returned to productive use through properly managed systems.

Planning for place

Central Louisiana should be part of the plan.

A system that works in a large city may not automatically work for smaller cities and rural communities.

  • Reasonable access for rural residents
  • Support for existing local programs
  • Safe and clearly identified collection options
  • Transportation between smaller communities and qualified processors
  • Straightforward public instructions
  • Transparent reporting about collected materials

With thoughtful planning, CENLA can become an important link in Louisiana’s circular materials economy.

A balanced view

It is a framework, not an instant solution.

EPR may help establish funding, responsibilities, and infrastructure. It still depends on the people, partnerships, and safeguards that make a program work.

EPR may help establish

  • Clear funding
  • Shared responsibilities
  • Collection infrastructure

It does not replace

  • Public education
  • Responsible participation
  • Local partnerships
  • Safety requirements
  • Program oversight
  • Convenient collection access

Each EPR program is different. Exact responsibilities depend on the covered product and the law or program creating the system.

  • Make information easier to understand

  • Build relationships with local and regional partners
  • Help CENLA prepare for future opportunities
For public education and planning

ReMine is not currently proposing a specific law or operating a battery collection program. This page is for public education and community planning.

One thing to remember

Responsibility should not end at the point of sale.

Residents, producers, retailers, governments, and recovery organizations all have a role. EPR helps create a clearer system for sharing that responsibility.
